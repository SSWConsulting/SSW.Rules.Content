A write-off is real money leaving the business. When the approval is vague ("sure, go ahead"), the person doing the work and the person approving it often walk away with different numbers in their heads. The first time anyone notices is when the write-off shows up in a report, and by then it is too late to have a say.

<endIntro />

The decision to write off time may well be correct. The problem is the communication: nobody else had the chance to confirm the amount, challenge the reason or plan around it.

## Be specific about what is being written off

Before any time is written off, agree on and record:

* **Who** the client or project is
* **How many hours** (or the dollar amount) will be written off, and a cap if the work is not finished yet
* **Why** it is being written off (for example, a goodwill gesture, an estimate overrun, or a mistake by the team)
* **Who** approved it

A general reply like "yes, that's fine" is not an approval of 5 hours. If the request is vague, ask a clarifying question instead of assuming. See [Do you get work approved before you do it?](/approval-do-you-get-work-approved-before-you-do-it)

## Include everyone who needs to know

Write-offs affect more than the two people in the conversation. Include the people who own the client relationship, the project budget and the reporting, so nobody finds out later by surprise. See [Do you CC relevant people and reply to all as a standard procedure?](/cc-and-reply-to-all)

If a request comes in without the right people on it, add them and ask for the specifics.

## Record it in the timesheet

Still [record all the work you do for a client](/do-you-follow-policies-for-recording-time), even if it is to be written off, and note the agreed amount and reason in the entry. That way the write-off is visible and traceable in reports.

Small goodwill write-offs follow their own guidance: [Do you sometimes write off small amounts of time to keep clients happy?](/do-you-sometimes-write-off-small-amounts-of-time-to-keep-clients-happy)
