Many developers only meet R&D as a section in the sprint review email, and never learn how their day-to-day work becomes part of an R&D claim. When work is not recognised as R&D at the time, the evidence is thin or missing when the report is written.

<endIntro />

Every piece of R&D work fits into one of three parts.

## 1. Core activity

The question the team is trying to answer, and how it will know whether it succeeded. See [Do you define your core activities for R&D?](/do-you-define-your-core-activities).

## 2. Experiments

An experiment is an attempt to do the thing the core activity set out to do. Experiments can be broken down in many ways.

Example core activity: *Establish whether AI can identify tasks discussed in video calls.*

* **Experiment 1:** Give the same call transcript to several LLMs and compare each result with the tasks you expected
* **Experiment 2:** Repeat the test with a non-English call, such as one recorded by an overseas office, and compare the results

Each experiment needs a measurable outcome. See [Do you make your R&D experiment outcomes measurable?](/make-rd-experiment-outcomes-measurable).

## 3. Supporting activities

Supporting activities are the work that makes the core activity possible. It is not the experiment itself, but it exists to support it.

Common examples:

* Research - how others run similar experiments, and what your benchmark should be
* Spikes - see [Do you know when to use the term SPIKE (Agile) or PoC (proof of concept)?](/spike-vs-poc)
* Documentation - on how to use the feature you are building or testing, and on the outcomes
* Testing - manual testing and unit tests that help show whether the core activity worked
