An experiment report that opens with its setup makes readers hunt for the result. A busy reader may forward "it is faster" without the caveat that the team tested one dataset on one machine, and someone else may treat it as a reason to deploy.

When reporting an experiment, spike, A/B test, benchmark, or AI evaluation, start with four short, labelled statements: **verdict, scope, limits, and decision**. Keep the answer and its boundaries together, then explain the method and show the evidence.

<endIntro />

## Put the answer and its boundaries in the opening

Use the opening of the report, PBI comment, or email to answer these questions:

* **Verdict:** What did you find? Give one sentence with the main outcome and the measure that supports it. "Inconclusive" is a useful verdict when the evidence cannot distinguish the options.
* **Scope:** What did you test? Name the compared versions or configurations, data, sample size, and environment. Distinguish repeated runs from independent users or examples.
* **Limits:** What remains uncertain? State important exclusions and caveats beside the result, including uncertainty in the estimate when available. Avoid a vague "more testing needed".
* **Decision:** What happens next? State the decision or recommendation, name its owner, and give a date or a condition for revisiting it. Label a recommendation as pending if nobody has approved it.

Keep each statement to a short paragraph; four labelled lines can wrap on screen. This applies [inverted-pyramid writing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/) to experiment reports: the main information comes before supporting detail. The four labels are a practical report template, not a statistical standard.

## Show what a narrow result actually supports

These excerpts describe the same fictional Northwind export benchmark. All timings, versions, people, and decisions are **example data**.

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    **Northwind export benchmark**

    We investigate the export process because the operations team waits for reports at the end of each day.

    We prepare a dataset, configure a test machine, and write a script to call both versions of the exporter.

    We run the script, collect timings, and review the results with the team.

    Version 2.4 is faster in our test. We should consider rolling it out.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="bad"
  figure="Setup buries the result, and the missing scope makes a rollout sound justified"
/>

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    **Verdict:** Northwind exporter v2.4 has a 25% shorter median export time than v2.3 in this fixture (600 ms versus 800 ms).

    **Scope:** 30 paired runs of v2.3 and v2.4 on one fixed 10,000-row synthetic orders dataset; Windows 11, 8 vCPU, 16 GB RAM, one export at a time.

    **Limits:** One dataset and one machine; no concurrent exports or production traffic. These are descriptive medians; no confidence interval is calculated.

    **Decision:** Keep v2.3 in production for now. Alex owns a test with 20 concurrent exports by 10 November 2026, then the team reviews whether to roll out v2.4.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="good"
  figure="The measured improvement, narrow scope, uncertainty, and owned next step stay together"
/>

The second opening supports a follow-up test without implying that production performance is proven. The 30 pairs are repeated timings of one dataset, not 30 different workloads. In a full report, put the method and raw timings immediately after this opening.

## Make uncertainty change the wording and decision

For statistical comparisons, report the effect size and an appropriate confidence interval when the analysis supports one. Include the confidence level and method in the details. [NIST explains how confidence intervals express uncertainty in an estimated mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm); do not apply its mean formula to a median or a conversion rate without choosing a suitable method.

If the estimate is too uncertain to support the proposed action, say so in the verdict and choose a smaller next step. If no valid interval is available, say that plainly instead of inventing a confidence percentage. A single successful spike can establish feasibility in its tested setup without establishing reliability in production.

Use the same four labels for an AI evaluation, but make the scope specific: model version, prompt and tool configuration, evaluation-set size, and scoring method. A score on that set does not establish quality on inputs you did not test.

## Keep the evidence after the opening

Follow the opening with the question or hypothesis, success criteria, method, results, and links to raw data or reproducible artifacts. Keep the report [with the research under the PBI](/do-you-record-your-research-under-the-pbi) so the conclusion can be checked later.

[Measurable R&D outcomes](/make-rd-experiment-outcomes-measurable) explain what evidence to collect. [Answering with yes or no](/answer-with-yes-or-no) makes a direct answer clear. This rule adds the reporting step: attach the tested scope and limits to the verdict, then connect that evidence to an owned decision.

Before sending, read only the opening and check:

* Can a reader tell what was found and what was actually tested?
* Would a forwarded excerpt still carry the caveat that changes the decision?
* Is the next action clear, with an owner and a date or review condition?
* Can a reader follow the details to verify the result?
