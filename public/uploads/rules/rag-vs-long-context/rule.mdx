"RAG is old school now" - and the person saying it is half right, which is the dangerous kind of right.

Here is the tell. Claude Code dropped its vector index in favour of agentic search, and its creator explained why publicly on the Latent Space podcast in **May 2025**. A competent dev shop recently discussed that decision as though it had happened a fortnight ago. Fourteen months of drift, in a team that reads the release notes.

The awareness lag is the real problem. People are still arguing about a two-way choice - retrieval or long context - that stopped being the question a while ago.

<endIntro />

## The 2026 question is three-way, not two-way

Retrieval-**as-a-pipeline** is genuinely obsolete as a default: chunk at 512 tokens, embed, take the top-k by cosine similarity, stuff the prompt, ship it. Retrieval-**as-a-capability** is more central than ever.

There are three options now, and the interesting question is not which one wins but what decides:

* **Long context** - put the whole corpus in the prompt and let the model read it
* **Vector retrieval** - embed the corpus, search it semantically, inject what matches
* **Agentic search** - give the model tools (`glob`, `grep`, an API, a query interface) and let it go looking

Three things decide between them: **corpus shape**, **cache shareability**, and **whether the answer has to be attributable**.

## Anthropic ships two of the three - deliberately

The company most often cited as having killed RAG runs both architectures simultaneously, and the split is not an accident.

**Claude Code uses agentic search.** Boris Cherny, its creator, on Latent Space (7 May 2025):

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    "we tried very, very early versions of Claude actually used RAG...eventually, we landed on just agentic search as the way to do stuff."
  </>}
  figurePrefix="none"
  figure="Boris Cherny, Latent Space, May 2025"
/>

His three reasons were performance ("it outperformed everything. By a lot."), no index to drift out of sync with the code, and no need to ship a customer's codebase to an embedding provider.

Anthropic later formalised this as **just-in-time context loading**, and described the result as a *hybrid*, not a vectorless model:

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    "Claude Code is an agent that employs this hybrid model: CLAUDE.md files are naively dropped into context up front, while primitives like glob and grep allow it to navigate its environment and retrieve files just-in-time, effectively bypassing the issues of stale indexing and complex syntax trees."
  </>}
  figurePrefix="none"
  figure="Anthropic, Effective Context Engineering for AI Agents, September 2025"
/>

The same post notes that the hybrid strategy "might be better suited for contexts with less dynamic content, such as legal or finance work" - which cuts against a tidy "code gets agentic search, documents get RAG" story. Read it as: **static corpora tolerate pre-loading; volatile corpora demand just-in-time.** That is a statement about volatility, not about file type.

**Claude Projects uses retrieval.** From Anthropic's own help centre: RAG "automatically activates when your project approaches or exceeds the context window limits", expanding capacity "by up to 10x", using a **project knowledge search tool** rather than loading everything at once. The best detail is the reverse gear:

<boxEmbed
  style="highlight"
  body={<>
    "If your project knowledge later drops below the context window threshold, Claude can automatically convert back to context-based processing."
  </>}
  figurePrefix="none"
  figure="Anthropic Help Centre - RAG for Projects"
/>

That is the split point shipped as a product behaviour. Corpus fits, use full context. Corpus does not fit, use retrieval. It switches in **both directions**, automatically, at the threshold. Anthropic did not pick a side - they shipped the boundary.

## Pick by corpus, not by fashion

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    "Everyone's moved past RAG, so we're putting all 40 documents in the system prompt for every user."<br /><br />
    Per-user permissions mean a separate cache per tenant. Each one amortises over that tenant's traffic alone, most tenants ask one or two questions a session, and you pay the cache-write premium on nearly every request.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="bad"
  figure="Bad example - choosing the architecture by what is fashionable"
/>

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    "The corpus is 60k tokens, identical for every user, and changes monthly. One shared cached prefix, no retrieval layer to build or keep in sync."<br /><br />
    Same technology choice - but now it is justified by corpus shape rather than by a podcast.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="good"
  figure="Good example - choosing the architecture by corpus shape"
/>

| Approach | Use when | Costs you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Long context** | Corpus under ~200k tokens, mostly static, one shared prefix across all users, questions need holistic reasoning, latency-tolerant. **Miss any one of those and retrieval wins.** | Tokens at volume; middle-of-context accuracy loss |
| **Vector retrieval + reranker** | Large unstructured prose - support docs, PDFs, policies, transcripts, wikis. Hybrid search plus a reranker is still the best quality-to-cost ratio for most production cases. **Do not over-engineer this.** | An index to build, sync and secure; staleness |
| **Agentic search** | Structured, live, permission-sensitive corpora with handles an agent can grab - codebases, filesystems, ticket systems, well-titled wikis | Latency (seconds, not milliseconds); tokens; weak on genuinely semantic queries where the answer uses different words (`retry` / `backoff` / `circuit_breaker`) |
| **Graph retrieval** | "What themes run across the whole corpus" questions, where no single query surfaces the answer | Expensive ingest - entity extraction costs LLM calls per write |

Real systems put a cheap router in front of several of these.

Agentic search's advantages are mostly about **what does not exist**: no index to sync, no staleness, permissions inherited from the access path, and queries a human can read in a log. That last pair is what actually decides enterprise architecture, and it is barely covered in the literature.

## Do the arithmetic before you argue

Cached input reads at roughly **a tenth** of normal input cost. Once the cache write has amortised, cached long context costs about `0.1 x corpus_tokens` per query, against roughly `retrieved_tokens` for retrieval. Setting those equal:

| Retrieved context per query | Long-context break-even corpus |
| --- | --- |
| ~5k tokens | ~50k tokens |
| ~20k tokens | ~200k tokens |

* **Under ~50k tokens of corpus** - just put it all in the prompt. Retrieval is not worth building.
* **50k-200k** - close enough that quality should decide, not price.
* **Beyond ~200k** - the gap compounds fast at volume.

**The variable that actually decides it in production is cache shareability, not corpus size.** Compute *queries per cache lifetime, per distinct prefix*. A cache write costs 1.25x normal input on the 5-minute TTL (2x on the 1-hour TTL), so below roughly two queries per cache lifetime you are paying the write premium on nearly every request and long context loses on every axis.

Two things drive that number below two:

1. **Per-user document sets.** Row-level permissions mean a cache per tenant, each amortised over that tenant's traffic alone. This is the single most underrated killer.
2. **Write frequency.** A corpus that changes hourly invalidates faster than you can amortise.

See [Do you follow best practices for prompt caching?](/do-you-follow-best-practices-for-prompt-caching) before betting an architecture on cache economics.

## A bigger window does not fix the middle

Accuracy across a context window is U-shaped: strong at the beginning and the end, weakest in the middle. Liu et al. found performance "significantly degrades when models must access relevant information in the middle of long contexts, **even for explicitly long-context models**".

Bigger windows make this worse, not better - the proportion of "middle" grows faster than the edges do. "We'll just use the 1M-token window" is a plan to hide the relevant paragraph in the worst possible place.

## Agentic keyword search gets most of the way there

The strongest empirical result available is an AWS-authored study (arXiv, December 2025) that held everything constant - same model (Claude 3 Sonnet on Bedrock), same datasets, same evaluation harness - and swapped only the retriever. The agentic side used `ripgrep-all` and `pdfgrep` in a ReAct loop with **no vector store at all**.

Relative to a vector-RAG baseline, agentic keyword search reached:

* **94.5%** of its faithfulness
* **88.1%** of its context recall
* **91.5%** of its answer correctness

And on FinanceBench - dense financial filings full of tables - the agent **beat** RAG on answer correctness, roughly 30% against 24.2%.

Read that honestly: agentic search generally landed *slightly below* the RAG baseline, with one dataset where it won. The paper's own conclusion is "over 90% of the performance metrics... without maintaining standing vector databases", and it flags real limits - degradation on large documents, weakness on ambiguous queries. It is an argument that the vector store is often **not worth its operational cost**, not an argument that it does not work.

## Arguments to avoid

These circulate widely and will get a post or a design doc picked apart.

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>"Anthropic killed RAG."</strong> They published a technique for <em>improving</em> it (contextual retrieval - prepend 50-100 tokens of document-level context to each chunk before embedding, cutting failed retrievals by up to 67% with a reranker) and still ship retrieval in their consumer product.</li>
      <li><strong>"Multi-agent beat single-agent by 90.2%, so agentic search beats vector search."</strong> That figure is about multi-agent orchestration on breadth-first research tasks, and cost ~15x the tokens. Different variable entirely.</li>
      <li><strong>"SWE-bench went from 1.96% to 80%+ once we dropped embeddings."</strong> Those systems differ in model quality, test execution, patch validation and iteration count. Attributing the whole gap to retrieval is a large leap.</li>
      <li><strong>"Lots of people still use RAG, so it's fine."</strong> The weakest argument available - it invites "yes, and they're behind."</li>
    </ul>
  </>}
  figurePrefix="bad"
  figure="Bad example - arguments that do not survive scrutiny"
/>

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    Argue the structural reasons instead: **corpus size, volatility, permissions, auditability, cache shareability.** None of those are fixed by a larger context window, and all of them are testable against your own workload.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="good"
  figure="Good example - arguments that hold up"
/>
