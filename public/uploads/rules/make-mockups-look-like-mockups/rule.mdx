Sometimes you show a client a big feature that still needs UI polish. If it looks finished, they assume it is finished, and the next question is "When does this go live?"

In those moments, you want the client to be painfully aware it is a mockup. A paper-like design does that at a glance, better than any disclaimer. Pair it with a prominent **Mockup** label inside the image, so the status survives being copied into a chat, slide or PBI.

<endIntro />

## Polish sets the wrong expectations

AI can [generate a pixel-perfect mockup](/generate-ui-mockups-with-ai) or [an interactive prototype](/ai-for-prototype-development) in minutes. When a big feature looks that polished, the client sees a finished product, even though the flow is not agreed and the UI has not been polished.

## Make it look like paper

Give the mockup textured paper, hand-drawn components and handwritten type. Nobody mistakes a sketch on paper for shipped software, so:

* **Expectations are set** - The client knows the UI still needs polish
* **Feedback stays on the flow** - People discuss structure, content and behaviour, not pixels
* **Changes feel cheap** - Nobody feels attached to a sketch

[Looseleaf](https://kevintngo.github.io/looseleaf/), created by [Kevin T. Ngo](https://x.com/kevin_t_ngo/status/2116929611196219764), is an example: its React components look hand-drawn on paper. The [component library](https://github.com/kevintngo/looseleaf) is [MIT-licensed](https://github.com/kevintngo/looseleaf/blob/main/LICENSE). You can use that visual approach without adopting a particular framework.

Keep the explicit label as well; a hand-drawn look can also be an intentional production design.

## Check that the mockup is unmistakable

* **Paper look** - Use textured paper, rough borders and handwritten labels. Keep text readable and controls understandable.
* **Mockup label** - Put a prominent **Mockup** label or watermark on every screen and export. It must remain readable when the image is scaled down.
* **Obviously sample data** - Use public sample data, e.g. Northwind customers, and never real customer records. Use `northwind.test` for demo URLs.
* **No brand polish** - Omit real logos, marketing photography and finished brand styling while exploring the concept.
* **Date and build state** - Add the date and **not built** so an old export cannot pass for current functionality.
* **PBI link** - Link to the Product Backlog Item beside the mockup and include its identifier in the export. Keep decisions and open questions with the work.

## When to use a polished design instead

The paper look is a deliberate choice, not a default. Use high fidelity when the visual detail is what you need to evaluate, e.g.:

* Final design sign-off, where reviewers need to see the intended appearance
* Brand or visual review, including colours, typography and imagery
* Usability testing of visual detail, such as contrast, spacing or control states

Label these artefacts **Design - not built**, with a date and PBI link. After design approval, **Approved design - not built** keeps approval distinct from implementation. Include realistic branding when it is part of the review.
