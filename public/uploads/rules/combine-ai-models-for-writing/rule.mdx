An AI-written product announcement can read well while promising something the product does not do. Asking the writer to approve its own draft gives you little reason to trust those promises.

For content you publish or reuse, **draft with one model, ask a second model to critique it against the sources, and let a human decide what changes to accept**. The extra review is useful when a missed fact could mislead readers.

<endIntro />

## Give both models the same source brief

Provide the audience, purpose, required facts, source material and length limit before drafting. Give the reviewer that same brief and the draft in a fresh conversation, without the writer's self-assessment. This lets it assess the text without being steered by an earlier verdict.

Use only material you are authorised to share with each provider. If the source cannot leave your approved environment, keep the review there or use a human reviewer.

For example, a Northwind release note has this brief (**example data**):

* Audience: existing workspace admins
* Release date: 12 November
* Feature: download orders as a CSV file
* Availability: Pro-plan workspace admins only
* Action: the admin selects Export; exports do not run automatically
* Length: no more than 50 words

## Ask a second model to find specific defects

Choose a different model, preferably from another model family when available, for a second perspective. Research on [self-preference in LLM evaluators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076) finds that models can favour their own outputs. This supports caution about self-review; it does not prove that switching models makes a draft correct.

Self-critique through a [critical agent](/critical-agent) or [Reflexion loop](/reflexion) can help polish a draft; factual approval still needs checks against the source.

Ask for defects and evidence rather than a score or a general "looks good". You can use this prompt with the brief and draft:

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    Review this draft against the supplied brief and sources. Identify unsupported claims, missing required facts and wording the audience could misunderstand. For each issue, quote the affected text, point to the supporting source passage and suggest the smallest correction. If a claim cannot be checked from the supplied material, mark it unverified. Keep optional style suggestions separate.
  </>}

/>

## Resolve disagreements against the source

A second model can repeat the first model's mistake or suggest a worse edit. Agreement between models is not verification. A human checks each factual correction against the source and accepts, rejects or investigates it. If the source is ambiguous, ask the person responsible for the fact before publishing; do not settle it by voting between models.

Use a word counter for length and a link checker for broken destinations. Neither a word count nor a working link proves a claim is true. Read the final wording after edits: a style improvement can accidentally change a promise or remove a restriction. See [how to avoid automation bias](/avoid-automation-bias).

The draft is ready when its factual claims have support, required facts remain, mechanical checks pass and a human approves the wording.

## Match the effort to the content

Use the extra review for announcements, documentation and other content where errors matter. A routine chat reply may only need a direct human read. If you have one model, a fresh conversation can provide another critique, but it still needs source checks and human judgement.

For R&D write-ups, see [how to gather evidence and review claims with AI](/prepare-rd-with-ai). It applies fresh-context critique and human citation checks to research records.

Choose models by the useful corrections they make on your content; [test model choices on your own use case](/choosing-large-language-models). Apply the [AI-writing checklist](/check-your-ai-writing) for tone and clarity as well as this factual review.
