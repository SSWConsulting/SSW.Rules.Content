Imagine a reviewer replying "AI slop" to a report because it contains long sentences and three-part lists. The writer is left defending their honesty, while the review offers no clear explanation of what should change.

Give feedback about specific problems in the text. If authorship matters, assess that question using evidence about how the work was produced.

<endIntro />

## What concern are you raising?

Identify the question before drawing a conclusion:

* **Writing quality** - Is a claim unsupported, a section repetitive, or the explanation unclear?
* **Authorship or assistance** - Who researched, outlined, drafted, translated, or edited the work?
* **Policy or disclosure** - Did the process meet a stated requirement?

A human-written draft can need substantial editing. An AI-assisted draft can be useful and accurate. Neither observation settles whether its production complied with a policy.

Length alone does not establish verbosity. Ask which material the reader does not need. Three-part lists, em dashes, formal language, and polished structure are ordinary writing choices. Their presence, individually or together, does not establish authorship.

## How should you give writing feedback?

Quote the passage and explain its effect on the reader. Distinguish errors from preferences, and propose a useful change. A house style can specify punctuation without turning a style deviation into an accusation.

Consider this illustrative sentence:

> The proposal covers scope, cost, and delivery — the three decisions we need before work starts.

## What can detection evidence establish?

Think of a sommelier tasting a wine without seeing its label. Familiar aromas may suggest a grape or region. Recognizing those clues does not guarantee an exact identification. Professionalism includes explaining both the clues and the limits of your judgment.

Even expert performance needs checking. In a [study of wine competition judges](https://wine-economics.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Vol.3-No.2-2008-An-Examination-of-Judge-Reliability-at-a-Major-U.S.-Wine-Competition.pdf), judges varied in how consistently they scored repeated samples from the same bottle. That study assessed wine ratings; it does not supply an error rate for AI detection.

Apply the same restraint to writing. AI-generated content can have recognizable patterns, and experience may help you notice them. Those tells are **suggestive, not conclusive**: human writers can use the same patterns, and AI-generated text can avoid them. Explain what makes you suspicious without claiming that your professional experience proves how the passage was produced.

> "This phrasing resembles AI output I have seen. It gives me a reason to ask about the writing process, but it does not establish that AI wrote it."

Some readers and tools perform well in particular tests. An [ACL 2025 study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267) reported strong results from a group of experienced readers on a defined set of articles. [RAID](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.674) found that detector performance changed across generators, domains, and attacks. Neither result validates every reader's intuition or every detector on new material. A [PNAS study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208839120) also found that the cues people use to spot AI-generated language are flawed.

Before relying on a tool, check its version, threshold, and evaluation conditions. Ask about false positives on comparable language, genre, length, and editing conditions. When that validation is unavailable, treat the score as a lead for a proportionate inquiry, not a conclusion. A displayed score is not automatically the probability that a writer breached a policy. Several tools or stylistic cues may share the same assumptions; agreement is not necessarily independent evidence.

## How do you handle a genuine process concern?

State which activity is in question and identify the policy or disclosure requirement that applies. Ask the writer privately for a proportionate account. Consider available notes, sources, or revision history alongside other relevant evidence.

These records can help, but none is infallible. Missing drafts are not proof of AI use. Being able to explain the argument does not prove sole authorship, and difficulty explaining it does not prove AI use.

Give the writer a fair opportunity to respond before making a consequential allegation. Record what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain. If it does not settle the question, say so. Keep any concrete writing feedback separate.
