Vague marketing claims like "much faster" or "industry-leading" tell the reader nothing they can verify. They read as opinion, not fact, so people discount them and AI answer engines skip right past them.

There is evidence for this. Nielsen Norman Group found that [79% of users scan pages rather than read them word-for-word](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) (Jakob Nielsen, 1997). 

A concrete figure like "200ms" survives that scan; a vague "much faster" does not.

<endIntro />

## Replace qualitative claims with numbers

Whenever you write a claim about performance, cost, scale, or results, ask *"what is the number?"* If you cannot answer, the claim is not ready to publish.

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    Our tool is much faster than the competition.
  </>}
  figurePrefix="bad"
  figure="Bad example - a vague claim with nothing to verify"
/>

<boxEmbed
  style="greybox"
  body={<>
    Our tool renders the report in 200ms — roughly 3× faster than Competitor X ([benchmark](https://example.com/benchmark)).
  </>}
  figurePrefix="good"
  figure="Good example - a specific number tied to a linked source"
/>

Swap subjective adjectives for measured facts:

* *"much faster"* becomes *"renders in 200ms — roughly 3× faster"*
* *"trusted by thousands"* becomes *"used by 4,200 teams"*
* *"saves you money"* becomes *"cuts hosting costs by 38%"*

## Pair every statistic with its source

A number is only as trustworthy as its evidence. Link each statistic to the benchmark, study, or dataset it came from so readers and AI search tools can confirm it and cite it with confidence.
